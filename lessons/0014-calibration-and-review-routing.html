Calibration & Review Routing

Mock 1 verdict: five of your six Domain-5 misses were this one task statement. The whole of TS 5.5 is a single question asked four ways: when can a machine's confidence be trusted to decide what a human doesn't look at? Answer: only after you've measured it.

Domain 5 Context Management & Reliability · 15% Covers TS 5.5 (+ TS 5.6 provenance) Mock evidence Q20 · Q26 · Q34 · Q42 · Q51 all missed

1. The aggregate mask

Every TS 5.5 stem starts the same way: a system reports a beautiful overall accuracy — 96%, 97% — and someone proposes automating on the strength of it. The trap: an aggregate is an average over segments you haven't looked at.

the segment a lawyer actually cares about is the one the aggregate hides

Read the picture slowly, because every TS 5.5 question lives inside it. The system processed contracts and someone measured accuracy once, over everything — that single number is the aggregate. But the work is not uniform: extracting a party's name is easy (it looks the same in every contract), while extracting an indemnification clause is hard (dense legal prose, varied wording). Easy fields are plentiful, so their 99% dominates the average, and the 71% field disappears into it. A segment is any slice you could measure separately — by field (names vs clauses), or by document type (invoices vs receipts vs purchase orders).

So the first calibration rule TS 5.5: verify accuracy by document type and by field segment before automating anything. "97% overall" is compatible with a 40% error rate on one document type — and automation applied on the aggregate silently ships that 40%. The aggregate isn't false. It's just answering a question nobody asked — "how good are we on average?" — when the decision at hand is "which slices are safe to automate?"

The trap you fell into on Q26 and Q34

Both times you picked the over-conservative option: "always human-review everything", "expand the review team". These feel safe, but the exam marks them wrong for a stated reason: they ignore the actual proposal's flaw (uncalibrated aggregate metrics) and abandon a legitimate goal (routing limited reviewer capacity where it matters). Rejecting automation outright is as uncalibrated as accepting it blindly. The correct options always fix the measurement, not the ambition.

2. What the right answer looks like — and why it is right

You said it directly: "I still don't know what kind of answers to choose." So here is the rule, stated as a rule.

The right answer, every time

On every TS 5.5 automation question, the correct option is the one that keeps the automation goal AND adds measurement — some combination of: validate per segment · calibrate confidence against labelled data · keep a stratified sample under review after automating. It is right because it is the only move that answers the question the decision actually needs: "which slices are safe?" Every other option either trusts an unmeasured number or abandons the goal.

Four option families appear on these questions, dressed differently each time. Learn to sort every option into its family and the question answers itself:

Add measurement "calibrate per field on a labelled set", "validate by document type", "keep stratified samples under review" CORRECT Converts unmeasured claims into measured instruments; automation proceeds exactly where the data says it's safe
Trust the model "auto-approve above 95% confidence", "have it self-assess and review the low-confidence ones" WRONG Raw confidence is uncalibrated — the model is fluent and self-consistent even when factually wrong, so the number measures nothing yet

Why the exam is built this way: the certification tests whether you can hold the business goal and the safety requirement at the same time. "Trust the model" fails safety. "Review everything" fails the goal. Measurement is the only option that satisfies both — which is why it is always the key, and why the over-conservative option is manufactured to tempt people who know the risks but not the instrument.

3. The calibration pipeline

Raw model confidence is a claim, not a measurement — you learned in L13 that it can route nothing on its own. Calibration is what converts the claim into an instrument:

confidence claims → measured thresholds → routing — with sampling as the audit that never stops

Read the two arrows that answer your missed questions:

  • Per-field thresholds (Q42). The model reported high confidence on both party names (99% actual) and indemnification clauses (71% actual). One global threshold cannot serve both — calibration against labelled data is done per field type, and that is what exposes the 71% field before automation ships it.
  • The dashed feedback loop (Q34, Q51). Automation is never "done": a stratified random sample of high-confidence, auto-approved output stays under review forever. That sample is what measures the true error rate and catches novel patterns the calibration never saw. Turning review fully off is the wrong reading of automation.

4. Spending scarce reviewers: stratify by risk

Q20's scenario: 150 API doc pages, capacity to review perhaps 50. You picked confidence-based self-routing — the unreliable proxy. The exam's answer allocates by consequence of error, not by the model's self-assessment:

~38 reviews cover the risk. Confidence scores never enter this decision — risk tiers do.

This is the second meaning of "stratified" in TS 5.5, and the exam uses both: stratify review allocation by risk tier (here), and stratify the ongoing audit sample by document type and field (section 2). Same word, two instruments.

5. Calibration has a domain of validity

Q51: three months of well-calibrated 90% thresholds, then a client submits contracts in an unusual two-column format. The system says 92% — above threshold. Trust it?

a threshold validated on one distribution says nothing about inputs from outside it

No. A calibrated threshold is a measurement of the formats in the labelled set. On a format the calibration never saw, the number "92%" is uncalibrated again — and the model may be systematically overconfident precisely because it doesn't recognise its own unfamiliarity. Correct handling: novel-format documents route to human review (building a labelled sample) until calibration covers them. Note what's wrong with the tempting alternative — raising the global threshold to 98% punishes the well-understood formats without actually detecting format-specific failures.

6. Provenance: the claim keeps its receipt

Your one TS 5.6 miss (Q6) belongs to the same family — trust must be traceable. In multi-source synthesis, the failure is attribution loss: findings get compressed into summaries, and by the final report nobody can say which source supported which claim.

Summary flattening

Search agent returns prose → analysis summarises the prose → synthesis merges summaries. Every hop discards sourcing; the report asserts orphaned claims. Conflicts get silently "resolved" by whichever value survived.

Structured claim–source mapping

Every finding travels as {claim, excerpt, source_url, published_date}, and the synthesis agent is required to preserve and merge the mappings. Conflicting statistics from credible sources are annotated with both attributions — never arbitrarily picked. Dates prevent temporal differences masquerading as contradictions.

The one-sentence version of TS 5.5

Measure before you trust; segment before you measure; keep sampling after you automate; and re-measure when the input distribution moves. Every correct option in your five missed questions is one clause of that sentence.
