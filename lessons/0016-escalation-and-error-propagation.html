1. The three valid triggers — and the two famous impostors

The blueprint recognises exactly three reasons for a support agent to escalate TS 5.2. Everything else is either "keep working" or a trap.

three doors in, two painted-on doors that exam distractors point at

Three refinements the exam probes:

  • "Escalate" and "resolve" aren't the only verbs. When tool results return multiple customer matches, the move is neither guessing by heuristic nor escalating — it's asking for another identifier. Clarification is the forgotten middle option. TS 5.2
  • An angry customer with a routine issue gets resolution, not transfer. Acknowledge the frustration, fix the duplicate charge — escalate only if they reiterate the request for a human after you've offered to fix it. But an explicit request up front is honoured immediately, without attempting investigation first.
  • Policy gap beats "complex". Competitor price-matching when policy only covers own-site adjustments isn't a hard case — it's a case policy doesn't answer. The agent improvising a commitment the business never made is the failure the trigger exists to prevent.
Your reflexes, patrolled

Mock 1 showed two habits: reaching for self-reported confidence (Q20) and for over-conservative blanket options (Q26, Q34). In escalation stems these appear as "escalate whenever the customer seems upset" and "escalate anything non-trivial to be safe." Both are scored wrong for the same reason as before: sentiment and blanket caution are unmeasured proxies for the thing that matters (can this be resolved within policy?), and blanket escalation destroys the 80% first-contact-resolution target the scenario states. The right answers name criteria — the three doors — and add few-shot boundary examples (L12).

2. Error propagation: the reliability view

You met structured errors in L06 from the tool designer's side. TS 5.3 is the same truth from the system's side, and one ladder covers every stem TS 5.3:

recover local → propagate structure → decide informed → annotate honestly

The discriminations each rung carries:

  • Rung 1 filters noise. The coordinator never hears about a timeout that a second attempt fixed. Escalating every transient upward recreates the boy-who-cried-wolf problem from L12's false positives.
  • Rung 2 is where "search unavailable" dies. A generic status hides everything the coordinator needs; the four fields (type, attempted, partials, alternatives) are what make rung 3 possible. And the L06 distinction still rules: an access failure is a failure — a valid empty result is an answer, and must not be dressed as either an error or a success-with-data.
  • Rung 4 is the honesty step people forget. If research proceeded with six of ten sources, the report says which topic areas are well-supported and which have gaps because sources were unavailable. That's L14's provenance instinct meeting error handling: the reader learns what the system doesn't know.
Domain 5 — and the course — in one line each

5.1/5.4: decide what must survive; give it a home the budget can't touch. 5.2: escalate on the three doors, never on unmeasured proxies. 5.3: recover local, propagate structure, annotate gaps. 5.5: measure before you trust; keep sampling after you automate. 5.6: every claim keeps its receipt.
